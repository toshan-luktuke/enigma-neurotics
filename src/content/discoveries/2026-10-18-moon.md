## The short answer

The Moon's average distance from Earth is increasing by about **3.8 centimeters a year**. That slow change is small enough to escape our eyes, but instruments can measure it.

## A round trip for a beam of light

Apollo astronauts left retroreflectors on the lunar surface. These devices return incoming light toward its source. Researchers on Earth send laser pulses at them and time the light's journey back.

Because the speed of light is known, the round-trip time gives a distance. Repeated measurements reveal how that distance changes. Getting a usable reflection takes careful aiming across the enormous gap between Earth and the Moon.

## What a slow measurement can tell us

One measurement tells you where something is. A long series lets you see how it moves.

The Moon looks much the same from one evening to the next, apart from its phases and position. Laser ranging gives researchers another view: a record precise enough to pick out a gradual change that a lifetime of casual watching would miss.
