## The short answer

The earthy smell after rain has a name: **petrichor**. Compounds in the ground, including plant oils and geosmin made by soil microbes, contribute to it. Raindrops help send some of that material into the air.

## A small splash with a surprising job

In 2015, MIT researchers filmed water drops landing on porous surfaces. The footage caught something too quick to notice outside: a drop traps tiny air bubbles as it hits. The bubbles rise through the water and burst, releasing a spray of much smaller droplets called aerosols.

The team proposed that this spray could carry the aromatic compounds behind petrichor. Their experiments also found that light and moderate rainfall produced more aerosols than heavy rain. A harder downpour didn't automatically make a bigger airborne spray.

That's a useful distinction. The research demonstrated the bubble mechanism and suggested how it could explain a familiar smell; it didn't claim every scent before or after a storm has the same cause.

## A smell before it had a name

CSIRO scientists Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas introduced the word petrichor in 1964. They investigated an odor that people already recognized, especially when rain followed dry weather. Their work traced it to oils held in rocks and soil and released by moisture.

The name combines Greek words for stone and the fluid of the gods. The process is more down to earth: moisture reaches the ground, compounds escape, and moving air brings them to your nose.

## Next time it rains

Pay attention to the first few moments of a shower. The scent is a reason to notice the ground as well as the sky. An ordinary drop can move material from one into the other.
